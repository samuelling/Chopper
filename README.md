# Ashland

A cel-shaded, first-person wander through a recreation of **Ashland State Park, Massachusetts** in late October. Fell trees. Drain the reservoir. Look at the leaves.

Run it (no build step):

```
python3 -m http.server 8000     # then open http://localhost:8000
```

## Packaging (Mac, Windows, iPad)

Not shipped yet, but prepared: the game is self-contained (no CDN), `npm run build` makes a clean `dist/`, it installs to an iPad home screen and plays offline, an Electron wrapper for Mac and Windows (set up for Developer ID signing and notarization) is in `packaging/electron/`, and an iPad Xcode project (Capacitor) is in `packaging/ipad/`. See [PACKAGING.md](PACKAGING.md) for the plan, signing and where to give it away, and [THIRD_PARTY.md](THIRD_PARTY.md) for licences.

## On a phone or tablet

Left thumb anywhere in the lower left: a floating stick. Drag the rest of the screen to look (scaled for big screens). The big button is the camera when your hands are free, and otherwise swings, casts, throws or shoots. **Use** says what it will do where you stand (Board, Pick, Stone, Stack, Swing, Tee off, Litter, Gate, Out) and sits you down when there is nothing else. **The tool button** cycles what you hold in one tap. Hop jumps (and lets go of the rope swing). Tablets get larger buttons, and can switch to keyboard and mouse in the pause menu.



Phones and tablets are detected automatically (or force it with `?mobile=1`; `?desktop=1` forces the desktop version). You get an on-screen joystick (left thumb), drag-to-look (right side), SWING / HOP / USE buttons and a sun button for the light (ringed by the day's progress, filling clockwise: gold by day, orange at sunset, blue after dusk, with a small blue tick where night begins; the season bar along the top edge is lost under a phone's notch), with the status card in miniature and a small map beside it on phones (under it on tablets), plus a lighter renderer: smaller shadow map, shorter draw distance, less leaf litter, lower pixel ratio. The title screen says it is best played on a desktop. Landscape works best.

## Game controllers and mice (`src/gamepad.js`)

Any controller the browser reports in the standard layout (Xbox, PlayStation, MFi, Switch Pro), on a Mac, PC, iPad or iPhone. Left stick walks (all the way over hurries), right stick looks. A hop (Dive while swimming: hold to go under), X use, B sit, Y next tool, RT the action (swing, cast and reel, throw, shutter; with empty hands it raises the camera), LT camera, LB flashlight, RB goggles, Start the phone, View/Select the menu, D-pad up the map, R3 plant. Flying the drone: left stick flies, right stick turns and tilts the camera, RT/LT climb and descend, A photo, Y AI spotter, B brings it back. Anywhere else (the phone, the menus, the season screens) a gold highlight moves between what can be pressed: d-pad or left stick to move, A to press, B to go back, right stick to scroll; the usage knob turns with left and right. Playing with a controller at a desk does not need the mouse captured; a click takes it back, and Esc pauses. On a touch screen the on-screen buttons fade while a controller is in use.

A mouse or trackpad on an iPad with touch controls works too: a click on the world captures it for looking (or drag to look if capture is refused), and clicking the on-screen buttons presses them.

Sound on iPhone and iPad starts with the first tap (iOS only allows it inside a tap), silent until the game fades it in. The upright phone has a **Put away** button in its corner, and the minimap starts on (nearby) on touch screens, under the status card. `?touchdebug=1` marks where each touch lands.

## About the place (what is real, what is not)

The map is built from **real OpenStreetMap data** (© OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL), exported with Overpass Turbo and stored in `data/ashland_osm.json` / `src/ashland_data.js`:
the reservoir shoreline and both islands, the dam, the beach, the boat launch, both parking lots, the loop trail and spur paths, service roads, and Cold Spring Brook below the dam.

The hills use **real USGS 3DEP lidar elevation** (public domain), 15 m grid in `src/ashland_dem.js`, measured relative to the reservoir surface (66.2 m) and vertically scaled by 0.6 (`VS` in `src/world.js`).

**Small features, from OpenStreetMap where it has them:** the spillway sits at the dam's east end, where OSM's spillway channel leaves the dam, and the shore trail crosses its head on a short wooden footbridge (OSM way 1106972362); below the steps its stream runs down to join Cold Spring Brook (way 893560130). The two picnic sites OSM marks south of the beach have tables. The little creek into the beach cove, with its footbridge where the trail crosses it, is not in OSM: it is traced from the lidar's drainage (a hollow coming down from the west to the cove just past the beach), so its exact line is a best guess. OSM's other footbridge, over a stream on the east side (way 1456153085), is outside the walkable park.

Where trees grow comes from **real NLCD 2021 land cover** (public domain, `src/ashland_landcover.js`): deciduous, evergreen and mixed forest, woody wetland, and cleared or developed land set tree density and the pine-versus-hardwood mix. Tree species follow the **real LANDFIRE forest type** (`src/ashland_species.js`) and the **real National Wetlands Inventory** (`src/ashland_wetlands.js`) decides where the swamps, marshes and ponds are: red-maple swamp, open marsh with cattails, boggy ground. Tree height follows **real LANDFIRE 2024 canopy height** (USGS/USFS, `src/ashland_canopy.js`), so taller stands in the real park have taller trees here. Individual tree positions within those areas are still scattered procedurally.

The lakebed follows the **real MassWildlife depth survey** of Ashland Reservoir (contours up to 48 ft, MassGIS; `src/ashland_bathy.js`), scaled with the same 0.6 vertical factor as the hills.

Pond life (sunfish, bass and painted turtles) is invented, cel-shaded like everything else, and only appears while the lake is full. The gray squirrels (they dart between pauses and scamper up the nearest tree if you get close) and the beaver-gnawed stumps along the shore are invented too: the stumps are scattered procedurally near the water, with no survey of real beaver activity behind them.

The edge of the playable park is a continuous chest-high dry-stone wall (a traced contour of the park-boundary field, stacked five courses high, too tall to jump), so the limit of the park is something you walk up to rather than an invisible barrier. The wall is an invented marker, not a survey of any real wall.

What is **not** real: the dam face and the area north of the survey, which are approximated, the gatehouse and spillway details, the helicopter wreck's exact position, and everything that sits on the land (individual trees, rocks, benches). Horizontal scale is `SCALE = 0.5` in `src/ashland.js` (set to 1 for true size; the lake is ~1.7 km long), and the map is rotated about 9 degrees so the dam runs along +x.

## The phone

**P** raises your phone: a Map tab (with heading), a Status tab (the harm/care gauge and figures), **Jobs** (Park Service Jobs: delegate tree work to the crew, who fell the trees over the next minute or two) and **Clyde**. Clyde is the AI assistant: at the end of each season you pick one of its offers (benches, a boardwalk, a kiosk, paperwork and so on), each showing its care and harm before you commit.

## Field Guide, camera and birdsong

You are a naturalist as much as anything. **V** raises a camera (narrow view, viewfinder); click to photograph the tree, bird, fish, turtle or squirrel in the middle of the frame. The first photo of each kind adds it to the **Guide** tab on the phone, with your own thumbnail. Songs and calls that you actually hear while walking (chickadee, cardinal, robin, wren, vireo, woodpecker, crow, jay, goose, hawk, spring peeper, bullfrog, cricket) are logged as you hear them, and can be replayed from the guide. Some species can only be collected by ear. Photos also go into an album (the Photos tab on the phone: a grid, then a full-size viewer with arrows, delete, and the species note; the newest 36 are kept, larger images at 480x320). The guide is saved in the browser (`ashland.guide`, `ashland.photos`) and cleared by "New game". The species are typical of a Massachusetts reservoir forest, **not** a survey of the real park; plants are not catalogued yet.

A gentle on-screen tutorial introduces the controls one at a time after the first landing (walk and look, hop, phone, camera, the rest), and clears itself as you do each thing. Esc opens the full list at any time.

## Being part of the place

- **Sit and wait (B):** crouch and stay still; the light moves on (mist, gold, dusk, night) and animals relax and come closer. Any step stands you up. The sun crosses the sky all day (east-north-east at dawn, due south at midday, west-north-west at dusk), so shadows swing round even while the light holds at one mood; sitting or lying in the hammock runs the day four times faster and the clouds race over like a time-lapse.
- **Deer** graze, freeze and watch you, and bolt with the white tail up; very still, they drift near. **Fox** (dawn, dusk, night), **chipmunks** (day), **frogs** on the shore (warm months), a **beaver** at dusk, **wood ducks** by day, **fireflies** at dusk in summer, **butterflies** and **dragonflies**, and **owls** and **coyotes** you mostly hear. All are invented behaviours of habitat-typical species, not survey data.
- **Weather:** spells of rain (and distant thunder in summer) and, in winter, snowfall. `?weather=rain` or `?weather=snow` forces it for testing.
- **Places (phone):** name any spot you stand on, just for you; they appear on your map. **Fog of war:** the map is dark until you have walked there. **Ruins** (cellar holes, a stone well, boundary stones; invented positions) are found by wandering near them. A **journal** logs it all.
- **Acts of care:** E picks up litter near the trails; every few pieces turns up a seed; G plants it as a sapling that takes many seasons to grow. The heron you keep seeing is noted.

## Fly-over

The title screen and season cards sit over a slow drone flight along the real reservoir's centre line, up one shore and back the other (`?noflyover=1` to turn it off). It stops when you click to begin.

## The shape of a playthrough

Part one is pure scenery: the first year, autumn through summer, with no consequences and no AI question anywhere. Each season opens on a card showing only its name in the title font; the first one waits for a click, each season ends by fading to black, and the next card fades up out of it, holds for a few seconds, and then the drone glides down to where you stand. After a full season of play (6 minutes by default, `?season_len=` seconds to change it) an interlude appears: what has changed, a footprint check-in the first time, and a choice (including making none). Part one is the whole first year: all four seasons turn with only the drone between them, and Clyde first appears at the end of summer. Consequences go live from the second year. Felled trees, lake level and your choices carry across seasons (each season is a page reload). "New game" on the season card resets everything. URLs with `?season=`, `?auto=` or `?dev=1` skip all of this and keep the old direct controls (season picker, T key, C choices with `?phase=2`).

## Seasons

Four seasons share one world: spring leaf-out and blossom shrubs, deep-green summer, the original autumn, and a snowy winter (bare cel-shaded branches, snow caps on pines, snow on the ground with a trail that stays trampled where you walk). Ground litter only shows in autumn. Switching season reloads the page at your current spot, so felled trees and lake level reset (a live blend is a possible later step).

Wildlife follows the season (for example no turtles or fish in winter, geese passing through in spring and autumn). The birds (chickadee, blue jay, great blue heron, Canada goose, red-tailed hawk) are species typical of the habitat, **not** from a park survey.

Turtles bask on logs and rocks and slip into the water when you run past.

## Pressure (art experiment)

**How help offsets harm.** Harm is what the land shows (fewer animals, a lower lake, faded colour, dying trees). Four things can push back, all set by the choices Clyde offers: **slower new harm** (pest scan, boardwalk, kiosk; stacks to 60%), **faster healing** (paperwork, forecasts, replanting; up to 3x the base rate), **healing now** (paperwork, replanting; small, one-off) and **upkeep that softens how harm looks** (benches, boardwalk, kiosk, cameras and so on; up to 40%, and the interlude shows lasting harm and visible harm separately). Rough balance from a hand-run simulation of the constants (not play-tested): Light use stays near zero, Medium use done with good picks levels off around 40-50% instead of ruining the park, Heavy and Very heavy still wreck it within about two to three seasons. Everything is invented and exaggerated on purpose.

**Time speed.** The pause menu has a *Time passes* setting (½×, normal, 2×, 4×; remembered on this device). It scales world time: the season clock, the harm and healing rates, the crew's work and the end-of-park timers, so a season weighs the same at any speed. Walking and the animals stay in real time.

## Endings

At the start you can give the **year you were born** (optional; only the year is kept, on this device; blank means the game assumes 40). Each game year is a year of your life, and the game counts down a rough population-average life expectancy (`src/life.js`; it is a clock, not a prediction). Every year opens with its title and your age ("Year Nine, age 53"). When the last year ends, **the game ends**: credits roll over a slow drone flight across the park. The other ending is **the reservoir draining for good**: if lasting harm sits at its limit for a minute the lake gives out (a warning appears at 90%), or if you open the drawdown gate at the dam and let it run dry. After a few seconds of empty lakebed the credits roll over a greyed, drained landscape. A third ending is **the woods going quiet**: when about four in five of the park's trees stand dead (by then wildlife is down by about half) for a minute, the credits roll at dusk over the dead woods, even if water measures kept the reservoir full. A warning comes at about two-thirds; the Park app shows the share standing dead. The two are separate: the lake reads the lasting damage and the water measures, while the woods read the damage softened by the woods' resilience (trees you plant, the shoreline buffer, upkeep choices; up to 55%). So water measures with no tree care can keep a full reservoir around dead woods, and a well-planted park can keep its woods while an unprotected reservoir drains. Harm builds steeply with use (rate ∝ use^1.6): with no care, the woods are lost after about 11 seasons at Medium, 2 at Heavy and 1 at Very heavy; Light use heals. Both endings are saved, so reopening the game returns to the credits, with *Begin again* (resets everything) or *Stay and watch*. For testing: `?end=life`, `?end=drained`, `?end=silent`, `?lifeyears=2`, `?cspeed=10` (credits speed).

A single dial, **Pressure** (0-100%, pause menu or `?pressure=0.5`), stands in for how heavily AI and data-center use leans on a place. As it rises the reservoir is held lower, colours fade, and fish, turtles, songbirds, squirrels and geese show up less. It is **not a measurement**: the mapping is invented and exaggerated on purpose so the cost is visible. The harm is **lasting**: pressure builds damage over minutes, and turning the dial down only lets the park heal over about an hour. Trees die back from the shore inward: a tree's turn comes mostly from how far it stands from the water (with a little luck), so the first to brown and lose their leaves are the ones at the water's edge, and the dieback spreads inland as harm rises. Leaves brown, then drop in clumps, until a bare grey snag is left standing; as the park heals, the leaves come back. `?damage=0.5` starts with existing harm. You tell the park about your AI use in three coarse ways (a knob on the opening menu and in Clyde's panel: how much of your Claude limit you have used, read off Settings → Usage on claude.ai; a Claude Code log can still be linked under "Advanced"; nothing touches the park until the second year): a **typical day** tier, a **daily check-in** ("how much did you lean on AI today?"), or by letting the game **count a Claude Code log** (`~/.claude/projects/**/*.jsonl`, pick files or, in desktop Chrome/Edge, pick the folder once and re-check on later visits after a permission click). The browser totals tokens per day locally (input + output + cache writes, not cache reads) and keeps only the daily level; thresholds are invented and shown on screen. Nothing is uploaded. The log format is not a published interface, and claude.ai chat usage can't be read at all.

**The data center** (`src/datacenter.js`). In the bleak endings (the reservoir drained, the woods gone silent, or a full life that leaves the park worn), once the credits reach the megaZINE page the drone climbs high over the park, out past its edge, and glides east until the data center over the ridge looms out of the haze: three long white halls with roofs crowded with chillers and fans, evaporative cooling towers breathing vapour, rows of diesel generators, a substation and power lines, a fence, red beacons blinking. The hum rises from far out over the woods and grows into something menacing as it circles slowly down: the low 60 Hz hum and its harmonics (the sound people who live near data centers complain about), a deep dissonant drone a tritone beneath, two fan tones beating, a broad roar of fans, a metallic whine, all through distortion. It swells and ebbs like breathing, the throb quickens and everything creeps sharp as you close in, and a buzzing band of upper harmonics and fan hiss carries it on phone speakers that cannot play the bass. A limiter keeps it as loud as it can be without clipping, and the game's compressor pushes the rest of the world down under it, then sweeps round wide to the east, drops to the ground through the fence and flies in at the open door of the middle hall: down its long central aisle in the dark between rows of server racks, their fronts flickering with green, blue and amber status lights, cold strip lights overhead, rising mid-hall so the whole cavern of racks spreads out below and dropping again, out the far door into the evening, round through the gate, slowing all the way to a soft stop on the sign: Clyde's icon, CLYDE CORP, Data Center, Ashland Campus, *making yesterday's tomorrow today*. The credits fade away, and it is just the building, the sign and the hum. It is built only when that flight starts. (Preview it with `?end=drained`, or `?dcflight=1&end=life` after any ending: an `?end=` link is a preview that saves nothing, and its buttons take you back to your game as it was.)

## Choices (phase two)

Once you have told the park about your AI use (or with `?phase=2`), a "C make choices" hint appears on the HUD card and **C** opens the pause menu with a choices panel. Every choice shows what it gives and what it costs before you commit. *AI* choices (survey the park, find a lookout, find a building site) add a one-time pulse of lasting harm and give a summary or a map and HUD marker. *Park* choices (harvest the nearest stand, hold off on AI today) act on the land. The benefits are placeholders until the real premise is set.

## Sound

All audio is synthesised with WebAudio, no sample files. The ambience changes by season (spring peepers and song, summer crickets and cicadas, autumn crickets, a few cicadas and crows, winter near-silence). The wind is kept low with a calm floor and soft random gusts every few seconds, after the Dry Stack soundscape. Footsteps depend on the surface: paving on the dam and ramp, gravel in the lots, sand on the beach, mud in wetlands, dirt on trails, leaves in autumn, crunchy snow in winter, grass otherwise. Only checked for errors in a headless browser, so tune it by ear.

## How it is built

- Three.js r186, vendored, ES modules, no bundler.
- Cel shading: toon materials with a stepped gradient, vertex-painted colour, and constant-pixel-width inverted-hull outlines.
- One heightfield drives everything (terrain mesh, water shader, player, props, trees).
- The forest streams in 64 m chunks with instanced trees and LOD; felled trees persist.
- Water is a depth-banded shader that follows `lake.level`, which is what the drawdown gate lowers.
- Sound is synthesised with WebAudio.

Debug: add `?auto=1` to skip the title screen, `&preset=1`, `&x=&z=&yaw=` to place the camera; `window.chopper` exposes internals.


## Terrain editing (for future building)

The ground is editable at runtime. `flattenArea(x, z, r, {y, blend})` in `src/world.js` levels a circular site and blends it into the surrounding slope, updating `groundY`, the shader height texture, the mesh normals and the slope-based colouring together (`editTerrain(x0, z0, x1, z1, fn)` is the general version). `ChunkManager.clearArea(x, z, r)` keeps trees, props, walls and their colliders out of the site and regenerates the affected chunks. `window.chopper.buildSite(x, z, r)` does both. The lake basin itself is left alone, so don't plan sites on the shoreline.

## The rowboat and fishing

A rowboat floats just off the boat ramp (`?ramp=1` starts you beside it). Walk up and press **E** to get in; you sit facing the bow. **W** pulls both oars, **S** backs water, **A** / **D** pull one oar to turn. There are no hands: the oars swing in the rowlocks by themselves. The shore stops the hull, you climb out with **E** where the water is shallow, and the boat stays where you left it. If the reservoir is drained the boat sits on dry mud.

Press **Q** until the rod is in hand (from the boat or from the shore), click to cast toward open water, wait for the bobber to dip, and click again to set the hook. Every fish is let go. Bites follow how much wildlife the park still has and how full the reservoir is, so a harmed or drained park gives you "Nothing. The water is quiet." Sixteen species turn up (sunfish, perch, crappie, both bass, pickerel, bullhead, shiner, sucker, carp, eel, and rainbow and brook trout in the cool seasons; some prefer dusk or certain depths), all typical of a Massachusetts reservoir, not a survey of the real one; the sizes are random. Each new species is added to the Field Guide (a Fish section) and every catch goes in the journal and the end credits.


### Touch
The phone button (top right) raises the in-game phone; turn the device upright and the phone (and Clyde, at the end of a season) fills the whole screen, turn it back sideways to put it away. Its **Tools** app holds what a keyboard does with keys: axe, rod or hands free, sit, pick up litter, plant a seed, the corner map, the choices. The big button swings, casts and reels (hold) or takes the photograph in camera mode; the joystick rows the boat and counters a fish's run; Hop held dives when swimming.

### Choices
Each of Clyde's options is a card: a one-line summary and two bars, care gained and lasting harm, each scaled across the options on screen so they compare at a glance (the exact figures sit beside the bars). Tap a card to see the detail (staff hours, illustrative dollars, side effects) and the button that commits.

### The park fund (Year Two on)
What AI help saves the park becomes a fund you can spend in the phone's **Park Fund** app: each choice's dollar figure, timber sold, and a per-season **dividend** that grows with your AI footprint (none, light, medium, heavy, very heavy: $0 / 1,500 / 4,000 / 7,000 / 9,000). It buys:
- **Seeds** (a sack of 25) and **nursery saplings** (8, three seasons along). G plants saplings first, then seeds. Every tree planted adds a little care and speeds the park's healing.
- **Ten lake measures in three sizes**, each a real practice. Small: low-flow fixtures at the bathhouse, rain gardens at the parking lot, smart-sprinkler rebates in town. Then: replanting the shoreline (riparian buffer; also plants a strip of young trees along the water near you), beaver dam analogs on the brooks, acoustic leak detection in the town mains, forecast-informed gate operations (Lake Mendocino stored about 20% more water this way in 2020), and a shade-ball cover (pays back only from the next season, costs harm up front, and fish bite less under it, after the MIT-led study of LA's shade balls). The AI-built ones cost a little harm too. Big: banking winter water underground (aquifer storage and recovery), and recycled wastewater for the data center's cooling (after Google's Douglas County, Georgia data center), the one measure that keeps working at any level of use, because it removes the draw instead of offsetting it.

**Clyde's nudges.** Every three seasons or so from Year Two (unless you are already at Very heavy), a notification from Clyde slides in at the top of the screen with what a little more AI would bring the fund, in the numbers of the moment: this season's dividend at your level against a heavier one, finds written up by hand that would pay up to five times as much, how far you are from the next thing in the Park Fund, visitors he could bring in, a lake measure the town keeps asking about. Tap it for the Park Fund. A temptation, offered the way these things are.

Together they add up to **lake protection** (0-100%), which reduces how much the harm lowers the reservoir. It holds fully at Light or Medium use, so enough of it saves the lake outright. Above Medium there is always a leak: the offsetting measures hold only in part at Heavy and not at all at Very heavy, and however much is installed (recycled cooling water included), protection covers at most about 60% of the drop at Heavy and 30% at Very heavy. The reservoir ending follows the lake itself (held at half its depth for a minute), not harm alone. The practices are real; their dollar costs and effects on this lake are invented.

### Catches, trophies and diving
- **Fish models** (`src/fishmodel.js`): each species has its own body outline, a painted skin (a perch's bars, a pickerel's chain, a brook trout's haloed red spots, a pumpkinseed's red ear dot), fins of the right kind, eyes and barbels. Landed fish are held up by the tail, head down, swinging from your grip, then let go.
- **Trophies**: rare, well past the usual size, for the bigger species (a 22-27 in largemouth, a 33-42 in carp, a 36-44 in eel). They bite more at dusk, in deep water, and in a park whose wildlife is doing well; they fight harder and tire slower, and are held up longer.
- **Catch log** (`src/catches.js`): every fish you land is photographed while you hold it, with its length, weight (from a length-weight curve per species), fight time, water depth, boat or shore, the nearest place you have named, season, year and light. The phone's Journal opens with a strip of catch cards to swipe through; tap one for the full card. Storage is local; when it fills, the oldest ordinary photos go first, never a trophy or a species' best.
- **Under the surface** (`src/underwater.js`): looking up while diving shows the world above through Snell's window, a rippling circle about 48.6 degrees across, with the surface a dark mirror outside it and a bright seam where it meets your eye line.

`?skip2=1` (testing): lands you at the end of Year One, a few seconds before Clyde first appears and Year Two begins. `?skipto=N` does the same for Year N (`?skipto=3`: the end of Year Two, just before Year Three). Both rewrite your saved run, then drop the parameter from the address.


### Wild things, pages and seats
- **Red fox** (`src/fox.js`): articulated like the deer (torso, turning head and ears, two-part brush, two-jointed legs in black socks); walk, trot and gallop; sniffs, sits with its tail curled round, hunts mice (head cocked, crouch, high leap, nose-first landing), stares when it sees you, then slips away.
- **Wild plants** (`src/plants.js`): thirty-four species (wildflowers, ferns, shrubs, a clubmoss, a lichen and the ghostly Indian pipe) in their own habitats and seasons (skunk cabbage and trout lily in spring, pickerelweed and water lilies in summer, asters and goldenrod in autumn, winterberry in the snow); photograph them into the Field Guide's Plants section, which hints when and where to look. They thin out as the park is harmed.
- **The park's page** (Year Two on): the first record of each species, moment or place goes on the park's (fictional) "Found at Ashland" page, and the visitors it draws pay into the park fund; rarer finds and places guests can go out to see pay more. Clyde writes it up in minutes; without AI you write it yourself and a fifth of the money arrives.

### The drone (`src/drone.js`)
A camera drone ($9,000 in the Park Fund, under "Just for fun": something to work toward, about two seasons of Medium use saved up, or one season of Very heavy use if you will take the harm for it). Q until it is in your hand, then the action button throws it up; your phone comes up sideways and fills the screen, and you fly first-person: two sticks on screen (left: climb and turn, right: fly), or WASD, Space and Shift, with the mouse turning and tilting the camera. Telemetry along the top (altitude, distance, speed, a battery that lasts three minutes). After every flight it has to charge back to full before it flies again: about seven minutes from flat, so it is a treat rather than a way to get around. It charges overnight, so each season starts full. It stays over the park: at the edge it says "Flying out of drone range" and drifts back in; there is a ceiling too. Q or Return brings it home and you are back on your feet, putting the phone away.

A species photographed from the drone is a speck: the park's page pays only a quarter of what a close-up does, and photographing it again on foot later pays the rest, so exploring in person stays worth it. Working for the park, it can still earn a little: an aerial photo of each named place, once, for the park's page, and one promo reel a season for a flight that takes in four places from up high. The **AI spotter** upgrade ($12,000) has Clyde watch the feed: now and then a lock closes in on a hidden thing in view, and a tight circle appears on your map. It adds lasting harm while it runs and more with each find; R switches it off.

### Forest fire (`src/fire.js`, `src/burns.js`) and binoculars (`src/binoculars.js`)

**Poachers and fire.** Poachers left in the woods are a fire risk whatever the state of the trees (a camp, a fire to keep warm, cigarettes): while they are there, and for a while after they slip away uncaught, a fire can start near their camp, about a 70% chance over a whole stay. Catching them with the drone's spotter ends it. **Slow fires cost the fund:** the trails close while it burns and visitors stay away, so when it is out the fund loses parking and donations by how long it went unfound and how big it grew (nothing for a quick find; a few thousand for one called in from town; up to $12,000 for one nobody found). **Sirens:** when a fire is called in (and when the police come for poachers) a siren wails far off, from that side of you.
From Year Two there is a small chance each season (none in winter) of a fire somewhere in the thick woods: 6% in spring, 12% in summer, 9% in autumn while the woods are healthy, about one fire every four years. The chance climbs steeply with the share of the woods standing dead (a quarter dead triples it, up to 75% a season). You smell smoke and see the column. Someone has to find it and call it in: walk close enough to see the flames, find the smoke's source through the **binoculars** (in the Q kit from the start; click raises them, the view narrows to two round lenses, and hills and thick canopy block the line of sight), fly the drone with the **AI spotter** (it locks onto a fire from far off), or pay a **lookout crew** ($5,000, Park Fund → Fire). Then the fire service puts it out, off screen. You cannot walk into it ("It's too hot"), but you can **cut a firebreak**: trees felled in a ring just outside the burning front slow it until the crew comes. A fire nobody finds is called in once the smoke is big enough to see from town, at about 60 m across in healthy woods; where the woods are dying, dead trees are kindling, and it runs faster and much further (up to 150 m across) before anyone calls it in.
**How exactly it was found decides how fast it is put out.** A fire called in comes with a fix, and a rough one means the crews search before they reach it while it keeps growing: through the binoculars it gets rougher the further off you are (a good fix from 200 m, crews there in about 25 s; little more than a direction from a kilometre, about a minute); on foot right by it, a good fix; flying the drone over it, a very good one (about 20 s); the drone's AI spotter, to the metre (about 10 s). The lookout crew is fair; a column seen from town, rough.
A burn leaves black, bare snags and a ragged circle of ash; ground plants are gone that season and come back the next, with **fireweed**. The ash fades over four seasons. It is fertile: trees planted in it start ahead and get twice the care. Burned trees count as dead in the woods meter. Flames are flat-coloured cones and the smoke is toon-shaded puffs, in the same cel style as the rest. For testing: `?fire=1` (a fire 6 seconds in) or `?fire=kindle` (as if the woods were dead), or press **9-1-1** during play to start one 100 to 250 m away, any season or year.

### Branch forts (`src/hidden.js`)
Three forts of big branches in the woods, there from the start, the kind people keep adding to for years: a **teepee** you can stand up in, a **lean-to** on a ridgepole between two trees, and a **big A-frame fort** with its ridgepole in the fork of a tree, a doorway and a log to sit on. Each stands in its own small clearing off the trail, with rumours pointing at them. Ashland really has forts like these: the teepee stands where you pinned one (42.24259 N, 71.46454 W, in the woods by the west shore south of the beach); the other two are placed by the game.

**Old Camp Winnetaska.** A Girl Scout camp stood on the reservoir's quieter west shore for decades until it closed in 2015 (the land is the town's now). The game has what is left of it in a clearing near the teepee: a campfire circle with log benches, a bare flagpole, a weathered sign with the letters gone, and the floors of a few cabins. The camp is real; the remains and their exact spot are invented. **The little island** has a fire ring and a few beer cans, as a hiker who waded out to it found.

### Things to do outdoors
- **Foraging** (`src/forage.js`): something to pick each season with E. Fiddleheads in wet ground (spring); highbush blueberries on the shore and black raspberries at trail edges (summer); apples from a few old orchard trees where the real Old Orchard Trail leaves the park on the east side, and hen-of-the-woods at the foot of big oaks (autumn); teaberries in the snow (winter). Each patch gives a few handfuls a season; a Field Guide section, *Foraged*, keeps count.
- **Winter on the ice**: the reservoir freezes. Walk out on it (you slide; it is slippery), cut a hole and **ice-fish** with the rod, or put on **cross-country skis** (in the Q kit in winter) for quick going in the trail's track. The rowboat is frozen in until spring.
- **The night sky** (`src/sky.js`): nights are longer now (the last quarter of each season), and sitting no longer hurries them. Stars, the Milky Way, and the constellations really up that season over eastern Massachusetts (the Big Dipper, the North Star and Cassiopeia all year; Leo in spring; the Summer Triangle in summer and autumn; Orion in winter). Look at one for a moment and its lines draw in, and it goes in the Field Guide under *Sky*. Shooting stars now and then, every few seconds in summer (the Perseids).
- **Night life** (`src/nightlife.js`): after dark the woods fill up with animals you never see by day, each in the Field Guide under *Night*. Raccoons along the shore; an opossum that falls over and plays dead if you walk up to it; a skunk that stamps and lifts its tail as a warning, and sprays you (the screen goes yellow-green for a while) if you keep coming; porcupines in autumn and winter; eastern coyotes that yip once and keep their distance; southern flying squirrels that glide from trunk to trunk; a barred owl on a low branch, calling "who cooks for you" from where it sits; big brown bats looping overhead; spotted salamanders crossing the trails on spring nights (the Big Night), American toads on summer trails, gray tree frogs trilling from low on a trunk, and spring peepers you can only find by following a single peep (all of these calls come from where the animal is, in stereo). Sweep the flashlight and their eyes shine back. Turn it off by a rotting log in late summer or autumn and it glows faint green (foxfire). Moths come to your light. New moments: eyeshine, the opossum playing dead, the skunk's warning, a flying squirrel mid-glide.
- **Goggles** (`src/goggles.js`), from the Park Fund's *Just for fun* list, worn over your eyes (O cycles through the ones you own, then off; Phone → Tools on a touch screen). Each runs on a battery that drains while you wear it (75 to 110 seconds) and charges while you do not; when it goes flat they go dark until it is back to 30%, and they flicker when nearly empty. **Thermal** ($10,000): the world goes cold grey, the open sky darkest, and every warm-blooded animal glows white up to 140 m away, by day or night (amphibians, turtles and fish are the temperature of their surroundings and do not show). For finding, not photographing: a photo through them pays 15%. **Night vision** ($14,000): amplified, grainy green, so you can prowl with the flashlight off; their infrared lamp is invisible to animals but still lights eyeshine. **Clyde Vision** ($6,000, plus $1,500 a season, taken from the fund the first time you put them on each season): AI glasses that tag every animal and plant near you with its name and distance (NEW for ones not yet in your Field Guide), and point to hidden things within 55 m. They add harm while they run, a photo taken with them on pays half, and the Field Guide marks the entry *spotted by AI* until you photograph it again with them off. The flashlight now has a cost too: a night animal with the beam on it bolts from twice as far (a raccoon at 14 m), while in the dark you can creep to about 4 m; an owl held in the light flies off, and a flying squirrel glides away.
- **Water life** (`src/waterlife.js`), in the Field Guide under *Water*: dive (hold Space or Dive) and the reservoir is full of small things, every one of them a Massachusetts freshwater species. Spinycheek crayfish (the native one) that rear up with their claws open and shoot off backwards; clouds of bullfrog tadpoles in the warm shallows; red-spotted newts that swim up for a breath; eastern elliptio mussels half-buried in the sand; caddisfly larvae in stick cases; dragonfly nymphs; magnificent bryozoans (jelly colonies on sunken branches, late summer and autumn); a snapping turtle resting in the deep that lifts its head to look at you, then walks off; and, in some hot summers, freshwater jellyfish (introduced from China, found in New England lakes). Anything under the surface can only be photographed from under it. On top: water striders, whirligig beetles and a northern water snake. Nothing in winter: the lake is under ice. Under water is now clear close up and fades with distance (about ten metres of visibility), instead of one flat tint. New moments: a crayfish with its claws up, a newt up for air.
- **Wary animals** (`src/stealth.js`): wild animals keep their distance, the way they do. Each has its own (a deer notices you much further off than a chipmunk), and how you move scales it: running at them, about half again as far; walking, as is; **sneaking** (hold Z, or push the stick only a little), a bit over half; standing still or sitting, closer still, and some come to you. At dusk they notice you less, and at night about half as far, except that a flashlight in their eyes doubles it (night vision does not). A deer that bolts at 28 m from a runner lets a walker come to 13 m, a sneaker to 8 m, and a sneaker at night to about 4 m. Deer, foxes, chipmunks, frogs, ducks, squirrels, songbirds, herons, geese, fish, turtles, the night animals, the water life and the legendary animals all go by it.
- **Charcoal grills** at the picnic areas (mass.gov: the park's picnic areas are "outfitted with charcoal grills and tables", and grilling is allowed only there): one at each of the two OpenStreetMap picnic sites south of the beach and two among the tables by the lot. E lights the coals, which glow and smoke for a few minutes.
- **Migrants** (`src/migrants.js`): birds that are only here while they pass through, at their real times of year (spring here is about May, summer July and August, autumn late October, winter January). Each season a few come through, with a note when they arrive, stay a while, and go on: ruby-throated hummingbirds at the flowers (spring and summer; a photo of one hovering is a moment), waves of yellow-rumped and magnolia warblers in the treetops, Baltimore orioles and rose-breasted grosbeaks back from the tropics, a common loon resting on the reservoir (with its wail), nighthawks over the water at dusk in late summer, kinglets, rafts of buffleheads, juncos, white-throated sparrows and, in winter, American tree sparrows. **Legendary migrants** (about one season in eight, with the legendary banner): snowy owl, Bohemian waxwings, evening grosbeaks and red crossbills in winter; sandhill crane, prothonotary warbler, tundra swan and American white pelican in spring (the pelican in summer too); rufous hummingbird, golden eagle, crane and swan in autumn. `?migrant=<id>` brings one in.
- **More birdsong**: fourteen more resident birds you hear around the park, each a synthesised sketch of its real song, by season, time of day and place: tufted titmouse (peter, peter), white-breasted nuthatch (yank, yank), mourning dove, red-winged blackbird and belted kingfisher by the water, song sparrow, eastern phoebe, gray catbird, American goldfinch (po-ta-to-chip), wood thrush and eastern wood-pewee at summer dusk, eastern towhee (drink your tea), pileated woodpecker and scarlet tanager. Every migrant sings its own song too (the white-throated sparrow's Old Sam Peabody, the oriole's flute, the junco's trill). Each one is logged in the Field Guide the first time you hear it, and can be played back there. Birding by ear now has titmice, towhees, song sparrows, phoebes, catbirds, goldfinches and wood thrushes to find as well.
- **Seeds and saplings in hand**: Q (or Tools on a phone) now includes them when you have any, and the action button or a click plants one where you look, one after another (G still works).
- **The noticeboard** (`src/noticeboard.js`): the back of the Ashland State Park sign by the beach is a cork board, and things get pinned to it as the seasons go: prints of your latest photos (the last three species, named in pencil), and pieces of paper about what is happening: smoke in the woods, shots heard off the trails, low water, a migrant or a legendary bird seen this week, a botanist's note about a rare plant, the hammock someone left out, pencilled leads, the season's park rules, and from Year Two a public notice about Clyde Corp.'s water withdrawal permit. Nothing opens; walk up and read it. When something new goes up while you are elsewhere, a quiet message says so.
- **The hammock** (`src/hammock.js`): from the third season, a note on the trailhead board says someone left a hammock strung up in the woods (finders keepers), and marks it on your map. Take it (E), and it is yours: Q (or Tools) brings it out, and the action button hangs it between two trees about three to five steps apart and you climb in. Lie back and look up and around: the canopy, the sky, the birds; the day drifts by four times faster (not at night), and animals take you for still. Any step gets you up; beside it, E gets you back in and the action button packs it up (walk off and you take it down as you go). `?hammock=1` makes it findable at once.
- **Legendary plants** (`src/rareplants.js`): about one season in seven (fewer as the park is harmed), one of the rarest plants of eastern Massachusetts is out somewhere in the park, in the kind of place it grows, and a rumour at the parking lot says so (but not where). Spring: small whorled pogonia (a federally threatened orchid) or painted trillium; summer: an American chestnut big enough to flower, large purple fringed orchid or pinesap; autumn: fringed gentian or pinesap; winter: spotted wintergreen above the snow. The first sight of one is a legendary find, and a photo of it pays like a legendary animal. `?rareplant=<id>` puts one out.
- **A porcupine asleep up a tree**: some autumn and winter days, a porcupine sleeps high in a tree near you, curled against the trunk, as they do by day. Photographing it is a moment. (`?porcupine=1`)
- **Birding by ear** (`src/birding.js`): now and then a cardinal, robin, house wren or red-eyed vireo perches nearby and sings every few seconds; the song comes from its side (stereo) and grows louder as you close in. Find it and photograph it: the bird for your Field Guide, and a moment.
- **Leaf-peeping**: in autumn a handful of red maples by the water flare brighter than the rest; a photo of one is a moment.
- **A grand stag**: some seasons (mostly in the autumn rut, never once antlers have dropped) the buck is a big one with a twelve-point rack. Legendary, with a sighting banner.
- **Your own cairn** at each skipping shore: E adds a stone, but it sways more the higher it gets, so wait until it stands still. Seven high is a photo.
- **The rope swing**: on a steep bank with deep water close. E grabs the rope, you swing out, Space (Hop) lets go; hang on and you drop back on the bank. Not in winter.
- **Swim to the little island**, and a **geocache chain**: the first geocache's logbook gives the coordinates of the next, on your map, through four more caches.
- **Disc golf** (`src/discgolf.js`): six holes on the east side (people play a course over that way) with tee pads, chain baskets and mown fairways. E at the tee, hold to wind up, let go: the disc fades left, clatters off trees and splashes in water (a penalty). Par 3 a hole.
- **Photos pay by distance**: an animal photographed within 8 m pays full value on the park's page, down to a quarter at 60 m (and from the drone, a quarter at most). A closer photo later pays the difference.
- **The rowboat across seasons**: if the season turns while you are rowing, you are still in the boat when it comes back (in winter the lake freezes around it, and you stand on the ice).

### Skipping stones (`src/skipping.js`)
Three **skipping shores** (invented names: Flat Stone Shore, The Skipping Ledge, Slate Beach), picked where a gentle bank of flat stones looks out on at least 50 m of open water. Finding one names it on your map and, from Year Two, puts it on the park's page. E picks up a stone. Hold the action and a gauge comes up: the arm's power fills (and fades if you hold too long) while a needle swings the stone's angle to the water between 0 and 45 degrees. Let go with the needle near 20 degrees (the real magic angle, from Clanet, Hersen and Bocquet in *Nature*, 2004) and the arm full: the best moment is the needle's way back down, just after a second. Each touch leaves a ring on the water. Ten skips or more leaves a chain of rings, and a photo of it before it fades is a Field Guide moment the park's page pays well for. Fifteen is mastery (the world record is 88). The game tells you what went wrong: too steep, too flat, not enough arm, or facing the bank.

### Legendary animals (`src/legends.js`)
The big, shy animals that really do live in Massachusetts but that almost nobody sees: a **black bear**, a **moose** (antlers in autumn and winter), a **bobcat**, a **river otter** swimming the shoreline in a run of dives, and a **bald eagle** on a dead tree by the water. At most one appears in a season, and only about one season in eight in a healthy park (fewer as harm rises); which ones follow their habits (no bears in winter, eagles mostly in winter and spring). It shows up somewhere ahead of you, crosses, and leaves; come too close and it goes. Seeing one brings up a *Legendary sighting* banner and a journal entry; a photo of one (the Field Guide has a Legendary section) is the biggest thing the park's page ever gets, $3,500 to $5,000 at full rate, because the local news picks it up. For testing: `?legend=bear` (or moose, bobcat, otter, eagle).

### Poachers (`src/poachers.js`)
Now and then (from Year Two; about one season in four, nearer one in two in autumn) two people with rifles slip into the thick woods off the trails for part of a season. You may hear a distant shot. On foot you never catch them: get within about 45 m and they hear you and are gone. Only the drone's **AI spotter**, looking down through gaps in the canopy, picks them out; then the Environmental Police deal with them at once and the fine, $2,500, goes to the park fund. The lock costs a little harm like any other. Invented. For testing: `?poachers=1`.

### More hidden things, in three shipments (`src/hidden.js`)
Thirteen more things to find, each the kind of thing New England woods really do hide: a glacial erratic, an old truck with a sapling through its engine, a rope swing over the water, a lone chimney, a family burying ground, an old bottle dump, a canoe left on the shore, a brass survey mark, a carved beech, a fairy house, an ice-house foundation, an anchor on the bottom, an old mill seat by the brook. They arrive over a life: the first set is there from the start, the second appears a third of the way through your life expectancy, the third at two-thirds (the game says when something new has turned up, and a lead points at it). All invented, placed with a fixed seed. On top of those, **one small find a year** from Year Two to Year Fifteen, whatever your life expectancy: a geocache, a shed antler, a hornet nest, a cairn, a birdhouse, an old sap bucket on a maple, a message in a bottle, a snagged bobber, a painted rock, an old lantern, a tree that swallowed a barbed-wire fence, a toy sailboat, a horseshoe on a tree, owl pellets under a roost. Each new year a rumour points at the latest one.

### Exploring (`src/explore.js`)
- **Named places**: reach a place and it gets its name on your map and a journal note. Real: the dam (built 1885, damming Cold Spring Brook), the beach, the boat ramp, Cold Spring Brook, the two islands. Invented: the names of three coves, three points, two hilltops and two swamps, picked from the shoreline shape, the terrain and the real wetland map.
- **By the water**: a beaver lodge off a cove and an osprey nest on a dead pine (found from the boat or swimming), a sunken skiff and an old farm road between stone walls on the bottom (found by diving), and the helicopter wreck. An osprey sits on the nest in spring and summer.
- **Leads**: dashed circles on your map, listed under Phone → Places. A note on the trailhead board starts you off; each season brings talk at the ramp or the beach; ruins point to more; from Year Two Clyde adds a tighter circle each season if you use AI, and the **Ask Clyde where to look** choice circles three hidden things at once for a cost in harm.
- **Moments**: photographing an animal mid-act files a separate Field Guide entry: a fox mid-pounce or sitting, a deer bounding or stamping, a beaver's tail-slap, wood ducks taking off, a chipmunk sitting up, a frog mid-hop.
- **Your pages**: the Journal has a place to write anything (field notes, poems), filed by year and season and kept even when you start over.
- **Seats**: B beside a bench or picnic table (the park's or ones you built) sits you on it, facing the view or the table.
- **Endings**: skip the credits to the start menu, or stay and watch with a menu button at hand; reloading after an ending opens the start menu.
